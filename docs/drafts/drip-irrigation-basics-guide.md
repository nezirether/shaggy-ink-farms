## 7. Introduction

If you garden in a place with long, dry, triple-digit summers — like here in Anderson, California (Shasta County, USDA Zone 9b) — drip irrigation isn't a luxury. It's the difference between a garden that produces and one you're constantly rescuing with a hose.

This is a beginner's guide written for someone who has **never installed irrigation before** and is going to do it themselves, on a budget. We'll cover what the parts are, what order they go in, how to size things for long water runs and a well, and how to set up simple systems for a family garden today and a strawberry field, cut-flower beds, and an expanding orchard down the road.

We're building Shaggy Ink Farms toward the 2027 season, and nearly everything we grow runs on drip off our well. We'll share the real setup and the real tradeoffs. None of this requires special skills — drip is mostly push-together fittings and common sense. Plan it once, build it in stages, and you'll use far less water for far better results.

## 8. Why drip irrigation

Drip puts water exactly where the roots are, slowly, with very little waste. In a hot, dry climate that matters more than anywhere.

- **Water efficiency.** Drip delivers water to the root zone instead of spraying it into hot, dry air, so far less is lost to evaporation and runoff than overhead watering. *Exact efficiency figures vs. sprinklers — `SOURCE NEEDED` (UC ANR / NRCS).* On a well and a budget, every gallon counts.
- **Healthier plants, fewer diseases.** Drip keeps leaves, fruit, and flowers **dry**, which dramatically cuts fungal problems like gray mold and mildew — critical for strawberries and cut flowers.
- **Deep, steady moisture.** Slow application soaks in deep instead of running off, encouraging deeper roots that handle heat better.
- **Fewer weeds.** Water goes to your plants, not the whole bed surface, so less of the ground sprouts weeds.
- **Hands-off consistency.** On a timer, drip waters early every morning whether or not you're home — exactly when plants want it in the heat.
- **Scales cleanly.** The same basic system grows from a single garden bed to rows of strawberries, flower beds, and orchard trees.

## 9. Components of a drip system

Every drip system, big or small, is the same chain of parts in the same order, from the water source out to the plants:

1. **Water source** (hose bib, well line, or valve).
2. **Backflow preventer** — keeps drip/fertilizer water from siphoning back into your drinking water. Important on well/potable systems. *Confirm local code/requirement — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
3. **Timer / controller** — automates watering (§14).
4. **Filter** — screens out grit that clogs emitters (§13). Non-negotiable, especially on a well.
5. **Pressure regulator** — drops household/well pressure to what drip needs (§12). Also non-negotiable.
6. *(Optional)* **Fertilizer injector** — for feeding through the system (a later add-on; see our Fertilizer Injector guide — forthcoming).
7. **Mainline** — the pipe that carries water to your beds/zones (§10).
8. **Drip line or drip tape** — the tubing with emitters that waters the plants (§11).
9. **Fittings, valves, end caps, and flush points** — to connect, zone, and clean the system.

A simple way to remember the order at the source: **backflow → timer → filter → regulator → mainline → drip.** Get that sequence right and the rest is just laying tubing.

## 10. Mainline pipe options

The mainline carries water from the source to your beds. Sizing it right matters most when you have **long runs** and a **well**, because undersized pipe loses pressure over distance.

- **½" poly drip tubing** — the common choice for small gardens and short runs; cheap, flexible, push-fit fittings. Fine for a family garden; can run short of pressure on long runs or many beds.
- **¾" or 1" poly / lay-flat** — better for **long runs and multiple zones** (our situation). Bigger pipe = less friction loss = more even pressure at the far end. Worth the small extra cost when you're running water a long way.
- **Buried PVC** — for permanent, long, high-flow mainlines (e.g., out to an orchard). More work to install; durable and out of the way. Use proper primer/cement and bury below damage depth.
- **Schedule note on long runs + well water:** know your well's flow (gallons per minute) and pressure before sizing pipe; a long run of undersized pipe can starve the end of the line. **When in doubt, go one size up on the mainline.** *Exact friction-loss / max-flow figures by pipe size — `SOURCE NEEDED` (manufacturer/irrigation tables).*

## 11. Drip tape vs drip line

These are the two ways to actually deliver water at the plant, and choosing the right one per use is the most useful decision in this guide.

**Drip tape** — thin-walled, flat tubing with pre-spaced emitter slits.
- Cheap, lightweight, easy to roll out down a row.
- Runs at **low pressure** (often ~8–10 PSI). *Confirm by product — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- Best for **annual production rows** you replant each year: vegetables, **strawberries, cut flowers.**
- Shorter lifespan (a season or a few), more delicate (sun, rodents, tools), and limited maximum run length.

**Drip line / emitter tubing** — thicker poly tubing with built-in **pressure-compensating** emitters at set spacings.
- More durable and reusable (multi-year).
- **Pressure-compensating** emitters give even output over **long runs and slopes** — a big deal for our long runs.
- Best for **permanent beds, perennials, and the orchard.**
- Costs more up front.

**Rule of thumb for us:** **tape** for annual rows (veg, strawberry, flower production), **pressure-compensating drip line** for permanent beds, long runs, and the orchard. You can absolutely mix both on the same system, each on its own zone.

## 12. Pressure regulators

Drip runs at **low pressure.** Your hose bib or well delivers **high pressure** (often 40–60+ PSI). Connect drip straight to that and you'll blow fittings off and split tape. **A pressure regulator is mandatory.**

- It drops incoming pressure to a fixed drip-friendly level — commonly **regulators rated around 10–25 PSI** depending on the system (lower for tape, higher for pressure-compensating line). *Match the regulator to your tape/line's rated pressure — `SOURCE NEEDED` by product.*
- It installs **after the filter** and before the mainline/drip.
- Match the regulator's flow rating to your system's flow; too small a regulator on too much flow won't regulate properly.
- On a well with variable pressure, a quality regulator keeps output steady so far beds get the same water as near beds.

If you buy one specialty part for drip, buy the right regulator (and the right filter). They prevent the two most common failures.

## 13. Filters

Drip emitters have tiny openings that **clog easily.** A filter is required — and on **well water**, doubly so, because wells often carry sand, sediment, or mineral grit.

- **Screen or disc filter** at the source, typically **150–200 mesh (~75–100 micron)** for drip. *Confirm mesh for your emitters — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- Installs **right after the timer/backflow, before the regulator.**
- **Well water extras:** if your well pumps sand or sediment, add a **sediment filter or sand separator** upstream of the drip filter so you're not cleaning the fine screen constantly. *Confirm sizing — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Clean it regularly.** Even the best filter needs rinsing; a clogged filter starves the whole system. Check it often early on to learn your water.

## 14. Timers

A timer turns drip from a chore into a set-and-forget system that waters at the best time (early morning) every day.

- **Hose-bib battery timer** — the beginner's friend: screws onto the spigot, runs on batteries, sets days/times/durations. Cheap, simple, perfect for one or two zones (a family garden). Start here.
- **Multi-zone controllers** — for larger systems with several valves/zones (strawberries + flowers + orchard each on their own schedule). Worth it once you outgrow a single timer.
- **Set deep and infrequent, adjusted by season.** Longer, less-frequent run times encourage deep roots; increase frequency in heat waves, back off in cool weather. *Exact run times depend on soil/emitter flow — start conservative and check soil moisture; `SOURCE NEEDED` for targets.*
- **Battery note:** dead batteries = no water in a heat wave. Mark a date to change them before summer.

## 15. Valves and manifolds

Valves and a manifold let you split one water source into **zones** you can run separately — which you'll need because a well can only supply so much flow at once.

- **Shutoff/zone valves** let you turn beds or sections on/off independently. Essential when total emitter flow exceeds what your well delivers at one time.
- **A manifold** at the source splits the supply into multiple zones (e.g., garden / strawberries / flowers / orchard), each able to have its own line, pressure, and schedule.
- **Why zone at all:** add up the flow of all your emitters; if it exceeds your well's GPM, you must water in zones rather than all at once. Zoning also lets perennials (orchard) and annuals (veg) run on different schedules.
- Keep it simple to start (one or two zones), but **put the manifold/valves where you can expand** later (see §23).

## 16. Irrigating vegetables

- Run **drip tape or ½" drip line down each bed/row**, with emitter spacing matched to plant spacing so every plant gets water.
- For wide beds, run **two lines**; for single rows, one line down the center is usually enough.
- **Water deep and less often;** let the top inch dry between waterings in cool weather, water daily in heat.
- Pair drip with **mulch** (see Weed Barrier & Mulch — forthcoming) to stretch every watering and keep soil cool.
- Replace tape as needed each season; it's cheap and meant to be semi-disposable on annual rows.

## 17. Irrigating strawberries

- Strawberries are **shallow-rooted and disease-prone** — drip is ideal because it keeps **berries and leaves dry** while holding steady soil moisture.
- Run **one drip line/tape down the bed center** for a single or staggered row; add a **second line** if a wider double row dries on the edges.
- Keep moisture **steady** — strawberries hate drying out or swinging wet-to-dry, which causes small or sour fruit, fast in our heat.
- See the [Strawberry Field Planning Guide](/learn/growing-guides/strawberry-field-planning) for bed design that the drip layout should match.

## 18. Irrigating orchards

- Trees want **deep, infrequent** water over a **wide root zone** — different from shallow veg watering.
- Use **pressure-compensating drip line** (even output over long runs/slopes) looped around each tree, or **multiple individual emitters per tree** spread across the root zone — not a single emitter at the trunk.
- **Add emitters as the tree grows.** A young tree needs a couple of emitters near the rootball; a mature tree needs them spread out under the canopy/drip line of the branches. *Confirm emitter counts/flow by tree age with UC ANR — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- Put the **orchard on its own zone** — its schedule (deep, infrequent) differs from the garden's, and the runs are usually longer (favor bigger mainline + pressure-compensating line).
- See Small Homestead Orchard (forthcoming) for layout and tree spacing.

## 19. Irrigating cut flowers

- Treat cut-flower beds like vegetable production rows: **tape or drip line per row,** emitter spacing matched to plant spacing.
- **Keep water off the foliage and blooms** — dry flowers mean better quality, longer vase life, and less disease (important for sunflowers and most cut flowers).
- **Steady moisture supports long, straight stems;** drought stress shortens stems and reduces quality.
- See [Growing Sunflowers for Cut Flowers](/learn/growing-guides/growing-sunflowers-cut-flowers) for the planting side.

## 20. Common mistakes

- **No pressure regulator.** Drip blows apart on full pressure. Always regulate.
- **No filter (or never cleaning it).** Clogged emitters, dead sections — especially on a well.
- **Runs too long for the tubing.** The far end starves; water is uneven. Upsize the mainline, use pressure-compensating line, or split into zones.
- **Ignoring your well's flow limit.** Running more emitters than the well can supply means weak, uneven watering. Add up flow; zone accordingly.
- **Overhead instead of drip in heat.** Wastes water, wets foliage, invites disease.
- **Mismatched pressure.** Tape on a too-high-PSI regulator splits; line on too-low pressure under-waters. Match the regulator to the product.
- **Not flushing the lines.** Sediment collects at the ends; open end caps and flush periodically.
- **No backflow protection on well/potable water.** A safety and sometimes code issue — install it.
- **Burying drip tape or leaving it exposed to rodents/sun unprotected.** Damages tape; route and protect it sensibly.
- **Watering shallow and frequent.** Trains shallow roots that fry in heat. Deep and infrequent.
- **Building a one-zone system with no room to grow.** Plan the source/manifold for expansion (§23).

## 21. Example small farm layout

A simple, expandable layout for a small farm on a well — the shape of what we're building:

```
WELL ──► backflow preventer ──► timer/controller ──► sediment filter (well)
        ──► drip filter (150–200 mesh) ──► pressure regulator
        ──► MANIFOLD with zone valves
              │
              ├─ ZONE 1: Family garden  → ¾" mainline → ½" drip line / tape per bed
              ├─ ZONE 2: Strawberry field → mainline → tape (1 line per bed, §17)
              ├─ ZONE 3: Cut-flower beds → mainline → tape per row (§19)
              └─ ZONE 4: Orchard (long run) → ¾"–1" mainline → pressure-comp.
                          drip line, emitters per tree (§18)
```

- **Zoning** keeps each section within the well's flow and lets perennials and annuals run on their own schedules.
- **Long runs (orchard)** get bigger mainline and pressure-compensating line so the far trees get the same water as the near ones.
- **Each zone** can be added when that part of the farm comes online (garden now; strawberries, flowers, orchard as they're planted toward 2027).
- *Confirm total flow per zone against your well's GPM before finalizing — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*

## 22. Budget starter systems

You do not need to build the whole farm system at once. Start with one zone, cheaply, and learn on it.

**Tier 1 — Family garden, one zone (the place to start):**
- Hose-bib battery **timer**, **filter**, **pressure regulator**, a roll of **½" mainline**, **drip tape or line** for the beds, fittings, and end caps. A modest, reliable kit. *Typical cost range — `SOURCE NEEDED` / varies by supplier and size.*

**Tier 2 — Add a production zone:**
- A **manifold + zone valve**, more mainline, and tape for the strawberry or flower beds. Reuse the same source parts.

**Tier 3 — Orchard / long runs:**
- Bigger mainline (¾"–1" or buried PVC) and **pressure-compensating drip line**, its own zone, more emitters as trees grow.

**Budget tips:** buy tape in bulk for annual rows; standardize on **one tubing size and one fitting type** so parts interchange; keep a small kit of couplers, goof plugs, and end caps for repairs; reuse durable parts (timer, filter, regulator, dripline) season to season.

## 23. Expansion strategy

Design the *source* for the farm you're building toward, even if you only plant one zone now.

- **Oversize the source side.** Put in a manifold and a filter/regulator that can handle more flow than your first zone needs, so you're not rebuilding the head of the system every time you expand.
- **Leave open ports** on the manifold for future zones (strawberries → flowers → orchard).
- **Standardize fittings and tubing sizes** across the farm so everything interconnects.
- **Match the controller to the plan.** A single hose-bib timer is fine to start; move to a multi-zone controller when you have several zones on different schedules.
- **Stage it to the 2027 build:** family garden first, then the strawberry field, cut-flower beds, and the expanding orchard each as its own zone as it goes in. We're documenting each install in the [farm journal](/journal) (Builds & Projects).
- **Keep a written map** of zones, line sizes, and emitter spacing — future-you will thank present-you when repairing or expanding.

## 24. Quick checklist

- [ ] Know your **water source**: well GPM and PSI (or hose-bib pressure).
- [ ] Install in order: **backflow → timer → filter → regulator → mainline → drip.**
- [ ] **Filter + pressure regulator are mandatory** (more so on a well).
- [ ] Choose delivery: **tape** for annual rows; **pressure-compensating line** for perennials/long runs.
- [ ] **Size the mainline up** for long runs; bury PVC for permanent orchard lines.
- [ ] **Zone** the system so each zone stays within the well's flow.
- [ ] Set the timer to **deep, infrequent, early-morning** watering; adjust by season.
- [ ] **Flush lines** and **clean the filter** regularly; check batteries before summer.
- [ ] Pair drip with **mulch**; keep foliage/fruit/blooms dry.
- [ ] Design the **source/manifold to expand**; standardize fittings.
