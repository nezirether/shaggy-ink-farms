## 8. Introduction

Most gardening advice is written for places with mild summers. Here in Anderson, California, that advice falls apart by the Fourth of July. We sit in USDA Zone 9b at the north end of the Sacramento Valley, where summer means weeks of triple digits, bone-dry air, and afternoon sun that can cook a tomato on the vine.

We're building Shaggy Ink Farms as a working family farm and getting ready for the 2027 season, and heat is the single biggest thing shaping what we grow and when. This guide is what we've learned from research and from our own beds: how to keep a garden producing through a real Northern California summer instead of watching it fry.

None of this is complicated. It comes down to choosing the right crops, watering the right way, keeping the soil covered and cool, throwing shade when the heat spikes, and — maybe most important — accepting that midsummer is a season to *hold and protect* a garden, not push it. Plant against the heat instead of fighting it, and you'll get far more food with far less heartbreak.

## 9. Why extreme heat changes gardening

Heat doesn't just make plants thirsty. It changes how they function, and that's why a garden can be well-watered and still stop producing.

- **Pollination fails.** Many fruiting crops drop their flowers when it gets hot enough. Tomato pollen, for example, loses viability when daytime temperatures stay high and nights don't cool down — so the plant looks healthy, blooms, and sets no fruit. *Tomatoes commonly stop setting fruit when days run above roughly 90°F and nights stay above roughly 75°F — `SOURCE NEEDED` (UC ANR / UC Master Gardeners for exact thresholds).*
- **Cool-season crops bolt.** Lettuce, spinach, cilantro, and the cabbage family read long hot days as a signal to flower and go to seed. Once they bolt, leaves turn bitter and the plant is done.
- **Plants protect themselves, not your harvest.** In extreme heat, plants close their stomata to conserve water and effectively pause photosynthesis during the hottest part of the day. Growth slows or stops even with plenty of water in the soil.
- **Sunscald and scorch.** Direct sun on exposed fruit (tomatoes, peppers) and tender leaves causes pale, blistered, sunburned patches.
- **Soil bakes.** Bare soil surface temperatures climb far higher than air temperature in full sun, stressing shallow roots and killing soil life near the surface. *Exact bare-soil surface temps `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Water disappears fast.** Hot, dry, sometimes windy conditions pull moisture out of soil and plants quickly, so the margin for error on watering shrinks.

The takeaway: in extreme heat your job shifts from *growing* to *protecting*. Keep plants cool, shaded, and steadily watered, and they'll coast through the worst weeks and pick back up when the heat breaks.

## 10. Crops that handle heat well

These are the crops that tend to keep going when the thermometer climbs. Treat this as a starting list and confirm specific varieties against a trusted seed source; performance varies by variety and microclimate.

**Vegetables / fruiting:**
- **Okra** — genuinely loves heat; one of the most reliable hot-summer producers.
- **Eggplant** — heat-tolerant and productive once established.
- **Peppers** — hold up better than tomatoes, though the hottest spells can still cause flower drop.
- **Sweet potatoes** — thrive in long, hot summers.
- **Southern peas / cowpeas (black-eyed peas)** — bred for heat; tough and productive.
- **Malabar spinach and New Zealand spinach** — heat-tolerant "greens" that stand in for lettuce/spinach in summer (not true spinach).
- **Amaranth (leaf)** — heat-loving edible green.
- **Armenian cucumber (a melon, eats like a cucumber)** — handles heat better than standard cucumbers.
- **Melons and watermelon** — heat is an asset, given enough water.
- **Summer and winter squash** — productive in heat with steady water (watch for mildew and pests).
- **Yard-long beans** — more heat-tolerant than common green beans.
- **Basil** — a heat-tolerant herb that pairs well with the summer garden.

*Specific heat-tolerant variety recommendations (e.g., particular tomato or pepper cultivars bred for fruit set in heat) — `SOURCE NEEDED` (Johnny's Selected Seeds, Territorial Seed, High Mowing variety data).*

**Practical note:** "handles heat" still means *given mulch, steady water, and ideally some afternoon shade during the worst spikes.* Heat-tolerant is not heat-proof.

## 11. Crops that struggle in extreme heat

These either stop producing, bolt, or decline once the long triple-digit stretches arrive. You can still grow most of them here — just in spring and fall, not at the peak of summer (see §16).

- **Lettuce, spinach, arugula, cilantro** — bolt quickly; turn bitter.
- **The cabbage family** (broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, kale, Brussels sprouts) — cool-season crops; poor quality and bolting in heat.
- **Peas** — a cool-season crop; done well before peak summer.
- **Tomatoes** — keep growing but commonly stop *setting* fruit during the hottest weeks, then resume when nights cool (see §9).
- **Standard cucumbers** — can get bitter and stressed; sensitive to heat and inconsistent water.
- **Beans (common bush/pole)** — flower drop and reduced set in extreme heat.
- **Many tender herbs** (cilantro especially) — bolt fast.

The honest version: in our Anderson summer, the cool-season crops above are a spring-and-fall game. Trying to grow lettuce in late July is a fight you'll usually lose.

## 12. Watering strategy

Watering is where most hot-climate gardens are won or lost. The goal is **deep, consistent moisture at the roots** — not frequent shallow splashes.

- **Water deeply and less often.** Deep watering pushes roots down where soil stays cooler and moister. Frequent light watering keeps roots shallow and vulnerable. *General principle — confirm depth/frequency targets for your soil with UC ANR; `SOURCE NEEDED` for specific numbers.*
- **Water early in the morning.** Early watering gets moisture to the roots before the day's heat and lets foliage dry, reducing disease. Evening is a second-best option; midday watering is the least efficient (more evaporation).
- **Use drip or soaker lines, not overhead.** Drip delivers water to the root zone with far less evaporation, keeps leaves and fruit dry, and is the backbone of efficient hot-climate watering. (We cover setup in our upcoming Drip Irrigation Basics guide.)
- **Adjust with the weather, not the calendar.** During a heat wave, beds may need water daily; in milder spells, back off. Check the soil a few inches down rather than guessing.
- **Mulch multiplies every drop** (see §13) — watering and mulch work together.
- **Containers dry out fastest.** Pots and raised beds heat up and dry faster than in-ground beds and may need water once or even twice a day in extreme heat.
- **Watch for wilting that isn't thirst.** Some plants wilt in the afternoon heat to conserve water and recover by evening even with moist soil. Check the soil before adding more water — overwatering in heat causes its own problems.

## 13. Mulch strategy

If you do only one thing from this guide, mulch. A good mulch layer cools the soil, slows evaporation, suppresses weeds that compete for water, and protects soil life — all the things extreme heat works against.

- **Use a 3–4 inch layer** of organic mulch — straw, shredded leaves, or untreated wood chips/bark for paths and perennials. *Confirm ideal depth with UC ANR; `SOURCE NEEDED` for exact figure.*
- **Keep mulch off stems and crowns.** Pull it back an inch or two from the base of plants to avoid rot and pest hiding spots.
- **Straw is the workhorse for vegetable beds** — clean, easy to apply, breaks down into the soil. Make sure it's straw (seed-free) rather than hay (full of seeds).
- **Mulch holds the gains from watering.** Bare soil between waterings can lose moisture fast; mulch dramatically slows that loss and keeps the root zone cooler.
- **Replenish as it breaks down.** Organic mulch decomposes (a good thing — it feeds the soil), so top it up through the season.

## 14. Shade cloth strategy

When a serious heat wave is coming, shade cloth is the difference between a garden that pauses and one that scorches. It's the most effective emergency tool for the worst weeks.

- **Use 30–50% shade cloth** over heat-sensitive crops during the hottest part of the day or during sustained triple-digit stretches. Lighter percentages (30–40%) for fruiting crops that still need light to produce; heavier (up to ~50%) for tender greens and new transplants. *Confirm percentage guidance with UC ANR / UC Master Gardeners; `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Shade the afternoon sun.** West and southwest exposure is the brutal one. Position cloth to block mid-to-late afternoon sun while letting cooler morning light through where possible.
- **Mount it with air gap.** Suspend the cloth on hoops, stakes, or a simple frame a foot or more above the plants so air can move underneath — draping it directly on foliage traps heat.
- **It protects pollination and fruit.** Reducing heat stress under shade helps sensitive crops keep setting fruit and prevents sunscald on tomatoes and peppers.
- **Temporary, not permanent.** Pull it during milder weather so plants get full light; reserve it for heat events and for protecting fall transplants started in late summer.
- **Improvise if needed.** Old sheets, row-cover fabric, or a strategically placed umbrella can buy a day or two in a pinch — but real shade cloth is cheap and reusable.

## 15. Soil protection

Healthy, covered, living soil is far more heat-resilient than bare, compacted dirt. Protecting the soil protects everything growing in it.

- **Never leave soil bare in summer.** Bare soil bakes, crusts, and loses moisture and microbial life. Mulch (§13) is the first line; living cover (densely planted beds, low groundcovers) is the second.
- **Build organic matter.** Compost and organic matter improve the soil's ability to hold water and buffer temperature swings. Work compost in before planting and use it as part of your mulch. *General principle — UC ANR.*
- **Avoid working soil in peak heat.** Tilling or digging dry soil in extreme heat destroys structure and dries it further. Disturb as little as possible during the hottest weeks.
- **Shade the soil, not just the plants.** Closely spaced plantings and mulch keep the soil surface out of direct sun, which keeps roots cooler.
- **Consider warm-season cover crops** for beds you're resting through summer — they shade and protect the soil and feed it when turned in (see our Warm Season Cover Crops guide).

## 16. Timing and succession planting

The biggest mindset shift for hot climates: **work the shoulder seasons.** In a mild climate, summer is the main event. Here, peak summer is the hard part to get *through*, and the real productive windows are spring and fall.

- **Spring:** plant warm-season crops (tomatoes, peppers, squash, beans, cucumbers) early enough to establish and start producing *before* the worst heat — but after frost danger. In Zone 9b that's a wide spring window. *Confirm local last-frost timing via `/learn/zones`; exact Anderson dates `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Peak summer (roughly July–August):** this is a *hold-and-protect* period. Keep established plants alive with water, mulch, and shade. Grow the heat-lovers from §10. Don't expect cool-season crops to perform.
- **Late summer → fall:** this is a second, often *better* growing season here. Start fall crops (brassicas, lettuce, root crops) in mid-to-late summer — frequently by starting seeds in the shade or indoors and transplanting out under shade cloth, because you're germinating cool-season crops while it's still hot. As the heat breaks in fall, they take off. *Confirm fall planting timing for Zone 9b with UC ANR; `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Succession planting:** stagger small plantings of quick crops rather than one big planting, so a single heat wave doesn't wipe out your whole harvest window. Our [Garden Planner](/plan/garden-planner) is built to help map this for your family and zone.
- **Mild winters are an asset.** Zone 9b winters are mild enough to grow a lot of cool-season food when hotter regions can't — lean into fall and winter as prime time.

## 17. What not to plant during extreme heat

Be honest with yourself in June and July. Planting these *into* the peak of summer usually means wasted seed, water, and bed space:

- **Cool-season greens:** lettuce, spinach, arugula — they'll bolt before they size up.
- **The cabbage family:** broccoli, cauliflower, cabbage, kale — start these for *fall*, under shade, not for midsummer.
- **Cilantro and other bolt-prone herbs** — they'll flower almost immediately.
- **Peas** — a cool-season crop; out of season in summer entirely.
- **New transplants of anything tender without protection** — if you must plant in heat (e.g., starting fall crops), do it under shade cloth with extra water, and ideally in the cooler evening.

The rule: don't start cool-season crops *for harvest* during the heat, and don't set out unprotected transplants into triple digits. Hold those beds with mulch or a warm-season cover crop until the timing is right.

## 18. Common mistakes

- **Watering shallow and often.** Trains roots to stay near the hot surface. Water deep, less frequently.
- **Watering in the evening or midday by default.** Morning is best; midday wastes water to evaporation; wet foliage overnight invites disease.
- **Leaving soil bare.** The fastest way to cook roots and lose moisture. Mulch everything.
- **Overhead sprinkling in heat.** Inefficient and disease-prone; switch to drip or soaker lines.
- **Fighting the season.** Trying to force tomatoes to set or lettuce to head in peak heat. Protect what's established; plant the right crops for the window.
- **Skipping shade cloth during heat waves.** A cheap, reusable tool that saves crops — many gardeners don't use it until they've lost plants.
- **Overwatering "wilted" plants that are just heat-coping.** Check soil moisture first; afternoon wilt with moist soil often recovers by evening.
- **Planting fall crops too late.** Because you have to start them *while it's still hot*, it's easy to miss the window. Start fall brassicas and greens earlier than feels natural, under shade.
- **Using hay instead of straw for mulch.** Hay carries weed seeds; straw doesn't.

## 19. Anderson / Northern California notes

This guide is written from Anderson, California, in Shasta County — USDA Zone 9b, at the north end of the Sacramento Valley. A few things specific to here and the surrounding area (Redding, Red Bluff, Cottonwood):

- **Our summers are long and intense.** Extended triple-digit stretches are normal, and the dry air and occasional hot wind pull moisture fast. Heat — not frost — is the defining constraint on the garden. *Typical summer high ranges and number of 100°F+ days `SOURCE NEEDED` (NWS/UC climate data for Redding/Anderson).*
- **Mild winters balance it out.** The same climate that punishes summer greens grows excellent cool-season food in fall, winter, and spring. Plan your lettuce, brassicas, and roots for those seasons.
- **Microclimates matter.** A west-facing bed against a fence or wall bakes; a spot with afternoon shade from a structure or an oak is gold in July. Map your yard's hot and cool spots before you place heat-sensitive crops.
- **Water is precious and sometimes restricted.** Efficient watering (drip + mulch) isn't just good practice here, it's often necessary. Check local restrictions during drought years.
- **This shapes our 2027 build.** Heat is a big reason we lead with drip irrigation, mulch, shade, and heat-smart timing as we get the farm ready — and why our strawberry field, cut flowers, and family garden are all planned around it. We're documenting what works (and what doesn't) in the [farm journal](/journal) as we go.
- **Use the local tools.** For frost dates, planting windows, and what to do this week, see [Find Your Zone](/learn/zones) and our [Anderson-area local guide](/learn/local/anderson).

## 20. Quick checklist

Heat-wave and hot-season survival, at a glance:

- [ ] Choose heat-tolerant crops for summer; save cool-season crops for spring/fall.
- [ ] Water deep, early in the morning, at the roots (drip/soaker, not overhead).
- [ ] Mulch 3–4 inches; keep it off stems; refill as it breaks down.
- [ ] Put up 30–50% shade cloth before/through heat waves, with an air gap above plants.
- [ ] Never leave soil bare; build organic matter; don't dig in peak heat.
- [ ] Plant warm-season crops early enough to produce before peak heat.
- [ ] Treat July–August as hold-and-protect, not plant-and-push.
- [ ] Start fall crops in mid-to-late summer under shade.
- [ ] Stagger small successions so one heat wave can't wipe out the whole harvest.
- [ ] Map your microclimates; keep heat-sensitive crops out of west-facing hot spots.
