## 7. Introduction

This is a planning guide, not a growing guide. If you want the week-to-week of *how* to grow strawberries in our climate — watering, feeding, mulch, renovation — read [Growing Strawberries in Northern California](/learn/growing-guides/growing-strawberries-northern-california). This guide answers the question that comes first: **how do you plan a small strawberry field that's actually worth the work?**

We're writing it from Anderson, California (Shasta County, USDA Zone 9b), where we're planning our **first production strawberry field for the 2027 season** — roughly **250 plants** in a handful of short rows, on drip, worked by our own family, on a tight budget. We're sharing the real plan and the real numbers as we figure it out, because most "start a strawberry farm" content skips the unglamorous parts: bed math, water lines, variety tradeoffs, and whether 250 plants can pay for themselves.

If you're a first-year grower, a homesteader, or a small-acreage farm planning strawberries in inland Northern California, this is built for you. Plan it once, on paper, before you buy a single crown.

## 8. Why strawberries

Strawberries earn a spot on a small farm for a few honest reasons:

- **High value per square foot.** Few crops return as much money or food from as little ground, which matters when you're working a small plot with family labor.
- **Strong, reliable local demand.** Fresh, ripe, local strawberries sell themselves at a farm stand, market, or driveway sign — they taste nothing like shipped grocery berries.
- **They fit a small-acreage, drip-irrigated, family-labor model.** A patch is manageable by hand; no specialized equipment required to start.
- **A real first-revenue crop.** For us, strawberries are the **first planned production field** — a sensible early income project while the rest of the farm comes together.

The honest caveats up front: strawberries are **perennial but short-lived** (plan to replace plants every few years), **labor-intensive at harvest** (they ripen fast and must be picked often), and **not a get-rich crop** at small scale. They're a good *first* production crop precisely because they're forgiving to plan and quick to return something — not because they're effortless.

## 9. Site selection

Pick the site before anything else; it's the decision you can't easily undo.

- **Full sun.** Strawberries want at least 6–8 hours of direct sun. *Confirm exact minimum with UC ANR — `SOURCE NEEDED`.* In our extreme inland heat, morning sun with a little afternoon shade in peak summer is ideal (see [Growing in Extreme Heat](/learn/growing-guides/growing-vegetables-in-extreme-heat)).
- **Drainage first.** Strawberries hate wet feet; standing water rots crowns and roots. Avoid low spots; favor a gentle slope or plan raised beds (see §11).
- **Avoid bad rotations.** Don't plant strawberries where tomatoes, peppers, potatoes, eggplant, or other strawberries grew recently — these share soilborne diseases (Verticillium, Phytophthora) that linger. *Confirm rotation interval with UC ANR — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Water access.** The site needs a reliable water source and a hose bib or line within reach for drip. Don't plan a field your water can't reach.
- **Wind and wildlife.** Note prevailing wind, and be honest about deer, gophers, and birds in your area — they shape fencing and netting decisions (see §18).
- **Close to the house.** At harvest you'll be out there constantly. A patch you walk past daily gets picked and tended; a far corner gets neglected.

## 10. Soil preparation

Strawberries reward good soil prep and punish shortcuts. Do this the season *before* planting where you can.

- **Test your soil.** A basic soil test tells you pH and nutrient baseline. Strawberries prefer slightly acidic soil, roughly pH 5.5–6.5. *Confirm target range with UC ANR — `SOURCE NEEDED`.* Most valley soils are workable; amend based on the test, not guesswork.
- **Add organic matter.** Work 2–3 inches of finished compost into the beds. It improves drainage in clay and water-holding in sand, and feeds soil life. *General principle — UC ANR.*
- **Fix drainage by going up.** Heavy clay (common here) drains poorly — raised beds or mounded rows solve most of it (see §11).
- **Clear perennial weeds completely.** Bermuda grass and other perennial weeds are far easier to kill *before* planting than after. Don't plant into a weedy bed (see [Bermuda Grass Control](/learn/growing-guides/bermuda-grass-control) — forthcoming).
- **Fertility: feed the soil, not just the plant.** Base amendments on your soil test; avoid dumping high-nitrogen fertilizer, which pushes leaves over fruit. *Specific fertility program — `SOURCE NEEDED` (UC ANR strawberry nutrient guidance).*
- **Time it.** In Zone 9b, prep beds in fall for winter planting (see §15), so the soil settles before crowns go in.

## 11. Bed design

Bed design is where you lock in spacing, drainage, and how easy the field is to work. Our plan uses simple, narrow raised rows sized for hand work and drip.

**Our bed spec (Shaggy Ink, 2027 field):**
- **Rows:** 1 ft wide × 20 ft long, **5–6 rows.**
- **Walkways:** 2 ft between rows — enough to kneel, pick, and set a harvest tote.
- **Raised or mounded** 6–10 inches for drainage in our clay-leaning soil.

**Why this design:**
- **Narrow (1 ft) beds** keep every plant within easy reach from the walkway — no stepping on beds, no compaction.
- **20 ft length** is easy to run a single drip line down and easy to mentally divide for counting/spacing.
- **2 ft walkways** are the honest minimum for comfortable harvest with a tote; tighter and you'll hate picking.
- **Raised beds** are the simplest fix for clay drainage and warm the soil a little earlier in spring.

**Footprint math:** 6 rows × 1 ft = 6 ft of bed, plus 5 walkways × 2 ft = 10 ft, so about **16 ft wide × 20 ft long ≈ 320 sq ft** total, with ~120 row-feet planted. That's a genuinely small footprint for ~250 plants — which is the point.

**Mulch/surface options:** black plastic ("plasticulture") warms soil, blocks weeds, and keeps berries clean but costs more and adds disposal; straw mulch is cheaper and soil-friendly but lets in more weeds. For a low-budget first field, decide based on weed pressure and budget — many small growers start with straw. *Plasticulture vs. matted-row tradeoffs — confirm specifics with UC ANR / commercial production guides; `SOURCE NEEDED`.*

## 12. Irrigation planning

Drip is the plan, and it should be designed alongside the beds — not added after.

- **Drip over overhead, always.** Drip delivers water to the roots, keeps leaves and berries dry (huge for disease — see §19), and is far more water-efficient in our heat. Overhead watering on strawberries invites rot.
- **One line per bed (sometimes two).** For a 1 ft single/staggered row, one drip line down the bed center usually covers it; a wider or double row may want two lines. Match emitter spacing to plant spacing so every crown gets water. *Confirm emitter spacing/flow with a drip supplier + UC ANR; `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Simple, cheap components.** A hose-bib timer, filter, pressure regulator, ½" mainline down the walkway, and ¼" or inline drip line per bed is a low-cost, reliable setup. (Full setup in [Drip Irrigation Basics](/learn/growing-guides/drip-irrigation-basics) — forthcoming.)
- **Plan run times by season.** Beds need more frequent water in heat, less in cool weather; mulch stretches every watering. Keep moisture steady — strawberries are shallow-rooted and hate drying out or swinging wet-to-dry.
- **Budget the water lines now.** Drip parts are a real line item (see §20). Lay them out on paper so you buy the right lengths once.

## 13. Variety selection

This is the planning decision with the biggest payoff, and it comes down to **when you want fruit.**

- **Day-neutral varieties** (e.g., Albion, Seascape, San Andreas, Monterey) fruit on and off across a long season as long as temperatures are moderate. **For a first small market or family field focused on fresh eating and steady sales, day-neutrals are usually the better choice** — they spread the harvest out, so you're picking sellable berries over many weeks instead of all at once. They slow in peak heat but pick back up.
- **June-bearing varieties** (e.g., Chandler, Camarosa) produce one big concentrated flush in late spring. Great if your goal is a large batch for preserving or a single market push; harder to sell fresh because everything ripens together.
- **Everbearing** sits between the two; generally less productive than modern day-neutrals.

**Our lean:** day-neutral, for spread-out fresh picking and simpler selling with family labor. *Confirm which specific cultivars perform best for inland Zone 9b with UC ANR / UC Davis and a reputable nursery before ordering — `SOURCE NEEDED`. Do not order on variety names from this guide alone.*

**Buy clean stock.** Order **certified disease-free** crowns/plugs from a reputable nursery — this is the cheapest disease insurance you'll ever buy. *Sourcing — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*

## 14. Plant spacing

Spacing is the lever between "more plants" and "healthier plants." Tighter spacing fits more crowns but increases competition and disease; wider spacing means fewer plants, bigger berries, and better airflow.

- **Day-neutral, raised-bed/plasticulture style:** commonly planted in **staggered double rows ~12 inches apart** within the bed, plants offset for even spacing. *Confirm exact spacing for your variety/system with UC ANR — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Single narrow row:** plants ~6–12 inches apart down the center.
- **Matted-row (June-bearing):** plants set wider (18–24 in) and allowed to runner to fill the bed.

To hit **~250 plants in our six 20-ft beds**, we need roughly **2 plants per row-foot** (250 ÷ 120 row-ft ≈ 2.08), which points to a **staggered double row** at about a 10–12 inch diagonal spacing — see the worked layout in §21. The honest tradeoff: that's a productive density but not loose; we'll watch airflow and disease closely and would rather drop plant count than crowd the bed if it gets tight.

## 15. Planting timeline

In Zone 9b, the planting window and the path to a 2027 harvest:

- **Bare-root crowns** go in while dormant — roughly **November through February.** *Confirm exact local window with UC ANR / UC Master Gardeners of Shasta County — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Plugs/potted plants** can go in late winter into early spring; get them established before heat.
- **Day-neutrals planted in late winter can produce their first season**, with the main payoff that summer/fall.

**Our backward-planned timeline toward the 2027 field:**
- **2026 (spring–summer):** choose and clear the site; kill perennial weeds; soil test and amend.
- **Fall 2026:** build raised beds, install drip, finalize variety order from a certified nursery.
- **Late 2026 / winter 2026–27:** plant dormant crowns (or early-spring plugs).
- **Spring–fall 2027:** establishment and **first real harvest** (day-neutrals producing across the season).
- **Ongoing:** plan to refresh plants every few years (vigor drops after ~3 years — see the growing guide).

*All dates are planning targets for Zone 9b; verify against the current season's local frost/heat timing via [Find Your Zone](/learn/zones).*

## 16. First year expectations

Be realistic, especially as a first-year grower:

- **Year one is establishment.** Even with day-neutrals that fruit the first season, expect a **modest** first harvest while plants build roots and crowns. Don't budget your season on year-one yields.
- **Pinch early flowers if advised for your type.** On some plantings (especially June-bearing), removing the first flush of flowers builds stronger plants for a bigger real harvest. *Confirm by variety/type with UC ANR — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Expect a learning curve on watering and picking cadence.** Strawberries ripen fast and must be picked every 1–3 days in season; missing a few days means lost or rotting fruit.
- **Some plants will fail.** A small percentage won't take. Order a few extra crowns to fill gaps.
- **The patch peaks in years 2–3.** Plan for the planting to hit its stride after the first year, then decline — that's normal and why you refresh on a rolling basis.

## 17. Common mistakes

- **Planting the crown at the wrong depth.** Crown buried = rot; roots exposed = death. Set the crown right at the soil line. (Detail in the [growing guide](/learn/growing-guides/growing-strawberries-northern-california).)
- **Poor drainage / no raised beds in clay.** The #1 killer here. Raise the beds.
- **Overhead watering.** Wet berries and leaves invite gray mold and mildew. Use drip.
- **Skipping the soil test and weed kill.** Planting into compacted, weedy, untested soil makes the whole field a fight.
- **Choosing the wrong type for your goal.** June-bearing for fresh sales = everything ripens at once and you can't sell it fast enough. Match type to how you'll sell/use the fruit.
- **Cheap or uncertified plants.** Saving a few dollars on stock can import disease that costs the whole field.
- **Overcrowding to hit a plant-count goal.** Density looks efficient on paper and causes disease in reality. When in doubt, fewer plants, better spacing.
- **Letting it dry out.** Shallow roots + inconsistent water = small, sour, or dead plants, fast in our heat.

## 18. Pest pressure

Plan for these honestly; manage them **IPM-first (prevention and least-toxic methods before any chemical), and follow UC IPM for our area.** This guide does not prescribe pesticides. *Confirm specifics and any product decisions via UC IPM — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*

- **Birds** — the most reliable way to lose ripe berries. Plan for **netting** over hoops from the start.
- **Slugs and snails** — hide in mulch, chew fruit. Reduce habitat, hand-pick, use least-toxic baits per label.
- **Spider mites** — thrive in hot, dusty, dry conditions (our summer). Steady water and humidity at the leaf help; monitor undersides of leaves.
- **Gophers and voles** — serious in field settings; consider hardware-cloth bed bottoms or traps. *Confirm methods with UC IPM — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*
- **Aphids and others** — monitor; encourage beneficials; treat only when thresholds warrant, per UC IPM.

## 19. Disease pressure

Strawberry disease is mostly **prevented by design** — clean stock, drip not overhead, airflow, drainage, and rotation. *Confirm all specifics with UC ANR / UC Davis plant pathology — `SOURCE NEEDED`.*

- **Gray mold (Botrytis)** — rots ripe fruit, worse with wet foliage and crowding. Prevent with drip, spacing, airflow, and prompt picking/removal of rotten berries.
- **Powdery mildew** — favored by crowding and certain conditions; airflow and resistant varieties help.
- **Crown and root rots (Phytophthora, Verticillium, red stele)** — soilborne and hard to cure. Prevent with raised beds, good drainage, certified clean plants, and avoiding bad rotations (no planting after tomatoes/peppers/potatoes/strawberries).
- **Leaf spots/blights** — usually cosmetic; manage with sanitation and airflow.

The pattern: you don't spray your way out of strawberry disease at small scale — you **design it out** up front and link to UC IPM for anything beyond prevention.

## 20. Profitability discussion

Honest version: a ~250-plant patch is a **real but modest** income project, best understood as "can it pay for itself and a bit more in a season, while feeding the family and building skill?" — not a salary. No success-story math here; just the levers and ranges, all of which **you must verify for your situation.**

**Rough costs to plan for (one-time + seasonal):**
- Certified plants: ~250 crowns/plugs (per-plant price varies by nursery/quantity — `SOURCE NEEDED`).
- Drip system: timer, filter, regulator, mainline, drip line (a modest but real upfront cost).
- Beds/amendments: compost, optional plastic/straw mulch, raised-bed material.
- Netting, totes, misc.
- Water (seasonal) and **family labor** (unpaid here, but real — harvest is the big time cost).

**Rough return levers:**
- **Yield per plant:** day-neutrals can produce on the order of **~0.5–1 lb per plant per season** in good conditions — `SOURCE NEEDED` (UC ANR / commercial production). At 250 plants and, say, ~0.75 lb each, that's a **ballpark ~190 lb** — but treat this as an illustration, not a promise; first-year and variable conditions cut it.
- **Price:** local/fresh strawberries command a premium over grocery; price by the basket/pound for your market — `SOURCE NEEDED` for current local pricing.
- **Sales channel:** farm stand / driveway / market / pre-order list. Selling fresh and local is where the margin is; you must be able to *move* the fruit during a short ripe window.

**The honest bottom line:** at 250 plants with family labor and a tight budget, a realistic goal for the first production year is to **cover your setup costs and earn a small profit while learning the crop**, then scale only if the selling (not just the growing) actually works. Strawberries are a good first revenue crop because the planning is simple and the feedback is fast — not because the money is large or guaranteed. **Run your own numbers with verified local figures before committing.**

## 21. 250-plant example layout (Shaggy Ink Farms, 2027)

A worked example you can copy or scale. Numbers are planning targets — confirm spacing with UC ANR for your variety.

**The field:**
- **6 beds**, each **1 ft wide × 20 ft long**, raised 6–10 in.
- **2 ft walkways** between beds.
- **Footprint:** ~**16 ft × 20 ft ≈ 320 sq ft**; ~120 row-feet planted.
- **Irrigation:** one inline drip line per bed off a ½" mainline running along an end walkway; hose-bib timer + filter + regulator.

**The plant math (staggered double row):**
- Target density ≈ **2 plants per row-foot** → 20-ft bed ≈ **~40 plants**; **6 beds ≈ ~240 plants** (round to the ~250 target; order ~10% extra for gap-fills → ~265 crowns).
- Staggered double row at ~10–12 in diagonal spacing; one drip line down the bed center (add a second line if a double row runs dry on the edges).

**Simple diagram (top view, one 20-ft bed):**

```
WALKWAY (2 ft)
┌───────────────────── 20 ft ─────────────────────┐
│ x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x ... │  ← row A (offset)
│   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   x   │  ← row B (offset)
│  ====================  drip line  ============== │
└──────────────────────── 1 ft wide ──────────────┘
WALKWAY (2 ft)
   … repeat for 6 beds → ~240–250 plants total
```

**If it feels crowded:** drop to **5 beds** or widen in-row spacing — fewer, healthier plants beat a packed, disease-prone bed. The layout is a starting point, not a mandate.

## 22. Quick checklist

- [ ] Choose a full-sun, well-drained site close to the house and within reach of water.
- [ ] Confirm no recent tomato/pepper/potato/strawberry rotation on the site.
- [ ] Soil test; add compost; fix drainage (raised beds in clay).
- [ ] Kill perennial weeds *before* planting.
- [ ] Design beds (1×20 ft, 2 ft walkways, 5–6 rows) and the drip layout on paper.
- [ ] Pick a type by your selling goal (day-neutral for spread-out fresh sales).
- [ ] Order **certified** plants from a reputable nursery; add ~10% for gap-fills.
- [ ] Plant dormant crowns in the local winter window; set crowns at the soil line.
- [ ] Plan netting (birds) and IPM-first pest/disease prevention from day one.
- [ ] Budget costs and run conservative profitability numbers with verified local figures.
- [ ] Expect a modest year one; plan to refresh plants every ~3 years.
